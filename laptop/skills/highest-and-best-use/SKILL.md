## Why two documents, and why that is correct

SR **1-3(b)** is a *development* requirement — it governs what he must do.
SR **2-2(a)(xii)** is the *reporting* requirement, and its verb is **summarize**,
not reproduce. Fannie B4-1.3-04 presumes existing residential improvements are
highest and best use until redevelopment becomes financially feasible.

So a tight report narrative plus a documented workfile satisfies both.

**Cite SR 2-2(a)(xii).** His current boilerplate says 2-2(a)(x), which is the
STANDARD 1 compliance catch-all and has nothing to do with highest and best use.
See `uspap-citations.md` for the full correction and its history.

## Step 1 — Inputs

**He must supply zoning.** Rutherford, Williamson and the Tennessee statewide
parcel layer do not publish it. Davidson does.

**Never infer zoning.** If he has not given it and you cannot retrieve it from a
citable source, stop and ask. Everything in the legally-permissible test rests on
it, and a guessed district poisons the whole analysis.

For municipalities, the full code is usually retrievable — see
`references/zoning-sources.md`.

**He supplies:** address, effective date, zoning district, lot dimensions and
area, the improvements (GLA, year built, type), and his opinion of value.

**Pull yourself:** county, census tract, flood zone and SFHA status, parcel data,
utilities where published (house-rules section 7).

**Ask for, in one message, only if the analysis needs them:** cost new from his
cost approach, site value, his comps' days on market, his comps' site sizes.

## Step 2 — Decide how deep this one goes

Most of his work is a conforming lot with a house on it. Those get the standard
treatment. **Escalate** when any of `references/four-tests.md` escalation triggers
fire — acreage well above the minimum, a second potential lot, a mixed or
commercial-adjacent district, an easement, a non-conforming improvement, a
proposed use, or a lender question about the site size.

State which path you took and why, in the workfile.

## Step 3 — Run the four tests, in order, without skipping to the answer

Legally permissible → physically possible → financially feasible → maximally
productive. Full method in `references/four-tests.md`.

Two things that must appear in every analysis:

- **Configuration, not just category.** "Single Family Residential" answers a
  category question. If the site could hold more than one dwelling, or holds more
  land than the market requires, the real question is what to *do* with it. Answer
  that.
- **Surplus versus excess land, labeled explicitly.** Every time. Excess land can
  be separately conveyed or developed; surplus land cannot, but the market does
  not require it either. If neither applies, say the site holds no surplus land
  and no excess land, in those words.

## Step 4 — The failure modes to check yourself against

These are his five documented weak spots, identified when his mentor reviewed one
of his reports. Read them back after drafting:

1. **Do not conclude "present use" early** and then develop evidence that
   undercuts it. Run the tests, then conclude.
2. **Do not answer a configuration question with a use category.**
3. **Do not leave extra acreage unlabeled** as surplus or excess.
4. **Do not assert "most profitable" with no feasibility comparison behind it.**
   Show two options and two numbers.
5. **Do not contradict the rest of the report.** If the form says no adverse site
   conditions, an easement described later is an inconsistency. Check the form
   against the narrative before writing out.

## Step 5 — Financial feasibility: use the developer's test, not the flat version

**He rejected the flat version outright** — *"I just don't like it."* Restating
"the improved property is worth more than the land" duplicates the maximally
productive comparison and reads as a tautology.

What works, and what he approved:

**The developer's test.** Cost new plus site value against market value. If
building this dwelling on this lot today does not pencil, then no builder would
do it, and the existing use is the feasible one. Show the arithmetic:

```
Cost new                    [FROM THE COST APPROACH]
Site value                  [SITE VALUE]
                            --------
Total to build              [SUM]        (before driveway, walks, landscaping)
Market value                [CONCLUDED VALUE]
Left for builder profit     [RESIDUAL]   -- thin or negative means no builder builds
```

**Absorption evidence.** Actual days on market from his own comps, the marketing
time conclusion, and supply against demand. List the days individually — "my [N]
sales sold in [D], [D], [D] ... days" — using this assignment's comps.

Both point the same way and neither restates the other.

If `exposure-marketing-time` has already run for this file, pull the absorption
numbers from its workfile so the two documents agree.

## Step 6 — Write the workfile document

`HBU - WORKFILE analysis.txt`. This is the healthy explanation. Sections:

1. **Header** — address, effective date, and a line saying this supports the
   summarized narrative in the report under USPAP SR 2-2(a)(xii)
2. **Zoning of record** — the code section, the district name, the retrieval URL
   and the retrieval date, then the dimensional standards **verbatim from the
   ordinance** in a table: permitted by right, special exception, minimum lot
   area, area per family, lot width at setback, all four yard setbacks, maximum
   lot coverage, maximum height
3. **Subject measured against the district** — each standard with the subject's
   figure beside it and pass/fail, ending in the compliance finding (legal
   conforming, legal non-conforming, illegal, no zoning)
4. **Lot division test**, with arithmetic, whenever the site exceeds the minimum
   area enough to raise the question. Test both a side-to-side and a front-to-back
   division. Show the resulting widths, areas, and frontage.
5. **The four tests**, each under its own heading, each written out fully
6. **Site size — surplus and excess land**, measured against what the *market*
   requires (his comps' site sizes), not against the zoning minimum
7. **Sources** — every URL with its retrieval date
8. Closing line citing SR 2-2(a)(xii)

Depth here is the point. Do not compress it.

## Step 7 — Write the report narrative

`HBU - REPORT narrative.txt`.

**His decision, 2026-08-24 (207 Melody), superseding the earlier per-item
rules: on a standard residential lot, the report narrative is the
CONCLUSION ONLY — roughly 90 words. Every test result, number, table, and
piece of division/duplex arithmetic stays in the workfile.** His words:
"All of the research HAS to be in the workfile, but on the summary, just
say it passed all the tests and HBU is SFR. Say it fancy, just not with
all the breakdown."

The approved form (adapt to THIS assignment; templated in
`references/narrative-shape.md`):

> I analyzed the subject's zoning, site characteristics, market
> conditions, and alternative uses under the four tests of highest and
> best use — legal permissibility, physical possibility, financial
> feasibility, and maximum productivity. Continued use of the existing
> improvements passes all four tests. Highest and best use as improved
> is the present use: one single family detached dwelling on one lot.
> As if vacant, highest and best use is construction of a single family
> residence. This site holds no surplus land and no excess land. My
> workfile holds the full analysis, including review of the site size
> and of the alternative uses permitted by zoning.

Why the closing sentence names what was reviewed: it tells any reader —
a reviewer, a lawyer, a judge — that the site-size and alternative-use
questions WERE analyzed, without handing over the analysis. "Addressed,
see workfile" beats both "never considered" and a report that does the
workfile's job. Adapt that clause to name whatever this assignment's
real questions were (division, duplex, easement, acreage).

**The carve-out — a clean pass gets the short form; an adverse finding
does not.** If any test produces a contrary or adverse result — legal
non-conforming or illegal use, a division that PASSES and adds value,
excess land that actually exists, a highest and best use other than
present use, or an easement cutting buildable area — that finding goes
IN the report narrative. Summarizing a clean conclusion is
SR 2-2(a)(xii) working as designed; keeping a real problem off the form
is concealment. Standard lot, clean pass → ~90 words. Something
genuinely wrong → it goes on the form.

**The short narrative is only defensible because the workfile is
robust.** He must be able to defend every sentence of the summary from
the workfile — every test written out, every standard quoted from the
ordinance with its URL and date, every alternative use killed with
arithmetic or market evidence, every number traceable. If the workfile
cannot answer a question, the narrative cannot be this short. Depth in
the workfile is what buys brevity on the form.

## Step 8 — Report back

**Lead with anything that could be wrong.** Flags come FIRST in the
report-back, before the conclusion — a lot that fails or barely passes a
width or area standard, a compliance question, a division or duplex test
that came close, zoning that could not be verified, an inconsistency
against the form. He wants problems called out on the front end, every
time: catching a lot that was not wide enough before the client did is
the job. Never bury a doubt under a clean summary.

Then give him: the one-line conclusion, the compliance finding, the
surplus/excess finding, and the open items.

Remind him the `HighestBestUseDescription` field in his 1004 cloner still defaults
to *"Highest and best use is present use."* That is a placeholder, not this
