# The four tests — method

Run them in order. Each test filters the set of uses that survives into the next.
Do not skip to the conclusion and then reason backward — that is documented weak
spot #1.

Analyze **as vacant** and **as improved**. On a form job with an existing house
the as-vacant analysis is short, but it still has to happen: it is what tells you
whether the improvements contribute or destroy value.

---

## SR 1-3(a) is bigger than most appraisers remember

The rule requires him to identify **and analyze** the effect **on use** and **on
value** of five things:

1. Existing land use regulations
2. **Reasonably probable modifications** of those regulations
3. Economic supply and demand
4. Physical adaptability of the real estate
5. Market area trends

Andersen's core teaching: **"Zoned R2" is an identification, not an analysis.**
Analysis says what R-2 permits and what that means *for this site*.

Nobody performs all five in depth on a $450 form job. Understand each well enough
to explain in one line why it was set aside.

---

## 1. Legally permissible

- Zoning district, cited to the code section, with the retrieval URL and date
- What the district permits **by right**
- What it permits by **special exception or conditional use** — these matter,
  because they are the alternative uses that have to be rejected on the record
- Private restrictions: deed restrictions, covenants, HOA rules
- Easements — a utility, drainage, or access easement can remove buildable area
- Environmental and regulatory constraints: floodplain, wetlands, historic
  district overlay
- Compliance finding: **legal conforming / legal non-conforming (grandfathered) /
  illegal / no zoning**

**Reasonably probable modifications.** A rezoning is only a legally permissible
use if it is reasonably probable, not merely possible. Evidence for probability
is a pattern of granted rezonings nearby, an adopted land use plan showing the
change, or a pending application. Absent that, say the current district governs.

**Rejecting a permitted alternative.** When the district allows something else —
town homes by special exception, a duplex, a manufactured home — reject it on
evidence, not assertion:

> [DISTRICT] also allows [ALTERNATIVE USE] as a special exception at [STANDARD],
> which this site could hold in theory. I rejected that option. Subject sits among
> [N] [PROPERTY TYPE] on lots of similar size. No [ALTERNATIVE USE] development
> exists in this market area, and no sale or listing in my search supports one. A
> special exception carries approval risk and no demonstrated demand.

## 2. Physically possible

- Site area, dimensions, shape, frontage, topography
- Utilities available — public water, sewer, gas, electric, or well and septic
- Soil and drainage where it matters; septic suitability on unsewered sites
- FEMA flood zone and SFHA status, with the panel and map date
- Access — frontage on a public road, or the easement that provides access
- Buildable envelope after setbacks, and the maximum coverage the district allows
  against what the improvements actually cover
- Any adverse condition observed on inspection

If the form says no adverse site conditions, nothing here may contradict it —
weak spot #5.

## 3. Financially feasible

Use the **developer's test** plus **absorption evidence**. He rejected the flat
"improved is worth more than vacant" version because it duplicates test 4.

**The developer's test.** Would anyone build this today?

```
Cost new (from the cost approach)          $ ______
Site value                                 $ ______
                                           --------
Total cost to build                        $ ______   (before site improvements)
Market value of the finished property      $ ______
Residual for builder profit                $ ______
```

If the residual is thin or negative, no builder builds it, and the existing
improvements are the feasible use. State that as the finding.

Run the same test for any alternative use that survived tests 1 and 2.

**Absorption evidence.** Do buyers want what already stands?

- Actual days on market from his own comparable sales, listed individually
- The marketing time conclusion
- Number of sales researched in the market area over 12 months
- Supply against demand, and the price trend

Two independent findings pointing the same way. Neither restates the other.

## 4. Maximally productive

Of the uses that survived, which returns the most? **Show the comparison with two
numbers** — weak spot #4 exists because this gets asserted instead of shown.

```
Keep the dwelling      property returns   [CONCLUDED VALUE]
Clear the site         site returns       [SITE VALUE]   before demolition cost
                                          ---------
Contributory value destroyed by removal   [DIFFERENCE]
```

Then name the alternatives that were blocked and why — zoning bars a second lot,
the market gives no support for town homes, no other use returns more.

Conclude as a **configuration**, not a category: *"one single-family detached
dwelling on one lot."*

## Surplus versus excess land — label it every time

Measure the site against **what the market requires**, using his comparable sales'
site sizes. Not against the zoning minimum.

| | Definition | Test |
|---|---|---|
| **Excess land** | Not needed to support the existing improvement, and **can** be separately conveyed or developed | Would a division yield a conforming, buildable lot? |
| **Surplus land** | Not needed to support the improvement, but **cannot** be separately conveyed or developed | Fails the division test, or has no independent access |
| **Neither** | The market requires a site this size | Site falls inside the range his comps show |

Write the finding in plain words even when it is negative:

> This site holds no excess land, because zoning bars a separate buildable lot.
> It holds no surplus land either, because the market requires a site this size.

## The lot division test

Run it whenever site area meaningfully exceeds the minimum — roughly 1.75x and
up, or any time a lender or reviewer might ask.

Test **both** divisions and show the arithmetic for each:

- **Side to side** — resulting widths against the minimum lot width at setback,
  and resulting areas against the minimum area
- **Front to back** — resulting areas, and whether the rear parcel has street
  frontage at all

A parcel that passes on area and fails on width does not divide. A rear parcel
with no frontage is not buildable. Say which standard failed, by name and number.

---

## H&BU gates comp selection

Comparables must share the subject's highest and best use. That is why a 2/2 is
not compared to a 4/3.5 — affordability is built into financial feasibility, and
the two properties draw different buyers.

If the H&BU conclusion here is inconsistent with the comps in the grid, one of
them is wrong. Flag it.

---

## Escalation triggers — when the standard treatment is not enough

Go deep, and tell him you are going deep, when any of these fire:

- Site area at two or more times the minimum, or acreage measured in acres
- Any plausible second dwelling or lot split
- Agricultural, commercial, or mixed-use zoning, or a commercial-adjacent site
- Legal non-conforming improvements
- An easement of any kind, especially one that crosses buildable area
- A proposed or under-construction improvement
- The lender or AMC asked about the site size, the acreage, or the outbuildings
- A use on the site that the district does not permit
- The improvements are obviously an over- or under-improvement for the area
- Rezoning activity nearby, or an adopted plan showing a different future use
